Spatial transferability: experimental set-up

Draft of 30 October 2026, for discussion. Nothing here is decided yet; the open points are listed at the end.

The idea in one sentence. Each model is trained in one country with all of that country's labels (K = 100 %) and then used, unchanged, in every other country. How much its score drops says how well it travels. The climate of the two countries says whether climate is the reason.

The questions

QuestionIn plain words
Q1How much does a model lose when it is used in a country it was not trained in?Train in Estonia, test in France: how far below a model trained in France does it score?
Q2Is the loss larger between countries with different climates?Two readings: Q2a, do countries in the same GAEZ zone lose less than countries in different zones? Q2b, which climate metrics does the loss follow? For each GAEZ metric of climate class, thermal regime, moisture regime and growing period: does the loss grow as the two countries differ more in it, and does it predict the loss better than plain distance in km?
Q3Do the foundation models lose less than the U-Net trained from scratch?Is a frozen GFM easier to carry to a new country than a model that learned everything from one country's labels?

How we answer them

What is trained and what is tested
  • Countries. The EuroCrops countries with labels for 2021, one year for all, so that no country is a different season (19 candidates, listed under Zones below).
  • The same pipeline in every country, as it runs for Estonia now: 2.24 km chips, twelve monthly Sentinel-2 and Sentinel-1 composites, and blocks of 4 x 4 chips split into training, validation and test blocks.
  • The same six models as the main workflow: TerraMind, THOR, Prithvi, TESSERA v2, AlphaEarth, all frozen with a trained decoder, and the U-Net trained from scratch as the baseline.
  • One training run per model and country, on that country's training blocks with all labels (K = 100 %), with the same 45 epochs and checkpoint rule as the main workflow.
  • Each trained model is then tested on the test blocks of every country, its own included. Testing in another country needs no new training, only prediction.

With 19 countries that is 6 x 19 = 114 training runs per seed, and 19 x 19 = 361 scores per model.

The same crops in every country

All countries use one fixed crop list, set before any result. A crop counts in a score only if both the training country and the test country grow it. The list itself is still open (see the end).

The scores
  • Score: Macro-F1 per pixel (main) and per parcel, as in the in-country results.
  • Home score: the score of a model trained and tested in the same country (the diagonal of the matrix).
  • Loss of a model trained in country A and tested in country B:

    loss = 1 − (score of the A model in B) / (score of the B model in B)

    0 means nothing is lost; 0.30 means it reaches 30 % less than a model trained in B itself. Comparing with B's own model keeps a country that is simply hard to map from looking like a failure to travel.

The climate of each country

All measured on each country's cropland only, with the GAEZ v5 layers (FAO and IIASA):

  • Zones, for Q2a. Two GAEZ layers, shown in the maps below:
    • Agro-climate: the GAEZ thermal climate. It groups countries by temperature, season length and how continental the climate is.
    • Agro-ecological zone (AEZ): the 33-class GAEZ zones, which combine temperature, moisture and soil. "Same zone" is then counted on each layer separately.
  • Climate metrics, for Q2b. 18 GAEZ metrics from the four GAEZ agro-climatic sub-themes: climate classification, thermal regime, moisture regime and growing period. Each country gets one value per metric, the average over its cropland. They are listed, mapped and explained under What the loss is correlated with.
  • Distance in km, between the centres of the two countries' cropland, to compare with.

What the zones show:

  1. The agro-climate makes three clear groups:
    • the oceanic west ;
    • the sub-continental east and north , where the Baltics sit with Finland, Czechia, Slovakia, Austria, Slovenia and Bulgaria;
    • the Mediterranean south .
  2. The AEZ puts the Baltics in the same zone as most of the oceanic west (temperate cool, wet ). By AEZ, Estonia and France are "the same zone"; by agro-climate they are not. The two GAEZ layers disagree on which pairs are close, which is why both are reported and why a continuous climate distance is needed beside them.
  3. The special classes hide the climate in the AEZ for some countries. Soil, terrain, irrigation or wetness cover 61 to 72 % of the cropland of Sweden and Finland, and about half of the Netherlands and Spain.
  4. Heat is the long axis. The heat sum runs from 2,200 °C days in Finland to 5,800 in Portugal.
  5. The Mediterranean countries also differ in water. Their growing period is about 80 to 135 days shorter than their warm season.

What the loss is correlated with

The study aims to find how strongly the loss follows each of the climate metrics below. They are the GAEZ v5 agro-climatic layers (FAO and IIASA), in the four sub-themes GAEZ groups them in, each averaged over every country's cropland.

GAEZ sub-themeWhat it describes (GAEZ's description, shortened)Metrics used (GAEZ code)

Notes on the choice:

  • Annual temperature amplitude is not among the GAEZ v5 layers. It can be computed from AgERA5 monthly mean temperatures (warmest minus coldest month); not done yet. The temperature profile is represented by the days above 5, 10 and 20 °C and the frost days.
  • Left out to keep the list short; they can be added: days above 35 °C (about 0 on the cropland of every country but Spain, where it is 2), the quarterly P/PET ratios, the frost indices.
  • GAEZ's catalogue files the heat sum under moisture regime and the deficit under thermal regime; here they follow the descriptions above.
How the correlation is measured
  1. One value per country and metric: the average over the country's cropland, weighted by GAEZ's cropland share. The start date is averaged around the calendar, so that December and January count as close. For the thermal climate, a country is its mix of classes.
  2. One difference per pair: for a model trained in A and tested in B, how much A and B differ in the metric, in the metric's own unit (for the start date, in days around the calendar). For the thermal climate, the share of cropland on which the two class mixes do not overlap: 0 for the same mix, 1 for no class in common.
  3. One correlation per model and metric: the loss of the 342 pairs against their difference, as a Spearman correlation ρ. Spearman works on ranks, so it does not assume a straight line. ρ near +1: the more the two countries differ in this metric, the more the model loses. ρ near 0: the metric says nothing about the loss.
  4. Is it real? A permutation test: the country names are shuffled many times and ρ is recomputed each time (a Mantel test). Pairs that share a country are not independent, so the usual test would be too optimistic.
  5. Climate or distance? Countries far apart usually differ in climate as well. The same correlation is computed for km, and each metric again after taking out what km explains (a partial Mantel test).
  6. One number per sub-theme: the metrics of a sub-theme are put on a common scale and combined into one difference per pair, which gives one ρ per sub-theme as well.
  7. Direction: the matrix is not symmetric, since EE to PT and PT to EE can lose different amounts. With signed differences (for example, tested in a warmer country than trained in), the same test says whether moving to a warmer or drier country costs more than the reverse.

What the metrics show:

  1. Many metrics move together, so the 18 carry about five separate signals. Spearman correlation between the differences of two metrics, over the 171 pairs of countries (gaez_metrics_correlation.csv):
    • warmth: mean temperature, heat sum and days above 10 °C, ρ = 0.95; PET and the start of the growing period follow them (0.72 to 0.83);
    • summer dryness: annual and summer P/PET, the deficit and rain days (0.74 to 0.88), joined by days above 20 °C;
    • amount of rain: precipitation and the Fournier index (0.84);
    • length of the growing period (LGD and LD1, 0.77 with each other), nearly independent of warmth (0.07 to 0.21);
    • winter cold: frost days and days above 5 °C (0.70).

    Winter P/PET and actual evapotranspiration relate only loosely to the rest (0.58 at most). With 19 countries, a correlation of the loss with one metric cannot be told apart from one with the other metrics of its family, so results are read per family.

  2. Warmer does not mean a longer growing period. Portugal and Spain have the most warm days but growing periods of 205 to 226 days, shorter than Belgium's 274: summer drought ends the season, and the deficit reaches about 700 mm there against about 100 mm in the Baltics. Because the two hardly correlate, the study can tell "warmer" apart from "longer season".
  3. The Mediterranean growing period starts in autumn: about mid-September in Tuscany, mid-October in Portugal and late November in Spain, against late February (France) to late April (Finland, Slovakia) everywhere else. This is the crop-calendar shift that the literature finds to be the main cause of loss across climates (transfer_literature.md). Our models see fixed calendar months, so it should show in the loss.
  4. Some growing periods are split in two. In Bulgaria the longest unbroken growing period is 144 of its 214 growing-period days, in Slovakia 197 of 238: a summer dry spell cuts the season.

Cost, roughly

  • Training. One model on all of Estonia at three budgets took 2 to 4 GPU-hours on the cluster, feature caches included. One run at K = 100 % on a country of Estonia's size is about 1 to 1.5 hours. That makes 114 runs per seed, a few weeks of the shared cluster for three seeds. France and Spain are many times Estonia's size, which is why a fixed-size sample of whole blocks per country is one of the open choices.
  • Data is the larger cost. Latvia's 11,500 chips took five days to export on the hub. Every new country needs its chips, its AlphaEarth tiles and its split.

Open before this becomes a protocol

  1. The crop list. A draft exists; whether cereals are kept by species or only by sowing season is open.
  2. Which countries. All 19, or a subset that still covers the three agro-climate groups and the heat gradient.
  3. How much of each country. The whole country, or a fixed-size sample of whole blocks.
  4. Label source per country. v11 or v2, which use different HCAT versions. Whether v2 Portugal is complete is unchecked.
  5. Climate values. GAEZ 2001 to 2020 averages (as above) or GAEZ's 2021 layers (planned).
  6. Which metrics. All 18, or one or two per family chosen beforehand. Testing 18 metrics for six models will find some correlations by chance, so either the tests are corrected for their number or the list is cut first. Annual temperature amplitude: compute it from AgERA5, or leave it out.
  7. The rule of 2 October, "transfer only within one GAEZ zone", turns from a filter into something the experiment measures. To be confirmed.
  8. Seeds. One seed first, three for the final grid.

Kept for later:

  • training on small anchor regions and testing at growing distance, across borders;
  • label-saving curves (a source country plus K target labels) on a few pairs;
  • other years.

3. Experiments that justify the pipeline

Each experiment varies one factor on one encoder and compares the variants fit by fit, on shared draws and seeds. The relative result backs the decision, so none is rerun on the current arms; experiments 1 to 3 ran on TerraMind or THOR with Sentinel-2 alone.

1
REPORT.md ↗

THOR token size: 160, 80 or 40 m

THOR v1 large on Sentinel-2, 733-chip sample, three seeds, 15 epochs.

Result. 40 m beats 160 m by about 0.05 Macro-F1 at every K and wins all nine paired fits; its cache is 16 times larger, about 25.5 TB for Estonia.

Decision. 160 m kept, for parity with TerraMind's 14 × 14 grid and a cache that fits a cluster node.

2
REPORT.md ↗

Rotations: 1, 4 or 8 D4 copies

TerraMind v1 large on Sentinel-2, all of EE_2021_rev, three seeds, 15 epochs.

Result. Eight copies beat the identity by 0.047, 0.061 and 0.070 at K = 100, 20 and 5 %; four sit between. Most of the gain comes through the checkpoint.

Decision. Training chips are cached in all eight flipped and rotated copies.

3

TerraMind v1 large on Sentinel-2, 733-chip sample, three seeds, 15 epochs.

TESSERA: its encoder on our composites, or the store's embeddings

Probe on EE_2021_rev_mini; fits on all of EE_2021_rev, three seeds.

Result. The encoder on our composites matches the store in a probe (0.371 against 0.373). In the main workflow frozen v2 is 0.018, 0.037 and 0.068 below the store arm.

Decision. TESSERA v2, frozen, on our composites: no 0.7 px offset, the same imagery as every arm.

TerraMind: Sentinel-2, Sentinel-1 or both

TerraMind v1 large, all of EE_2021_rev, three seeds, 15 epochs.

Result. Both sensors beat Sentinel-2 alone by 0.026, 0.054 and 0.025 and win every fit; Sentinel-1 alone is 0.02 to 0.07 below Sentinel-2.

Decision. Every model reads every sensor it was pretrained on.

Fine-tuned or frozen encoder

Every encoder with weights, all of EE_2021_rev, 45 epochs, one seed.

Result. TESSERA gains 0.04 to 0.08; TerraMind, THOR and Prithvi about 0.01 in validation, their test difference set by the checkpoint. Fine-tuning costs 4 to 15 times the frozen fit.

Decision. Encoders stay frozen in the main workflow; fine-tuning is reported as an insight.
