# Novelty claims and core methodological requirements

**Positioning document for the MSc thesis *Geospatial Foundation Models for Transparent Agricultural Monitoring under Label-Scarce Conditions*** (D. Reyes, ITC, University of Twente). Compiled 11 September 2026.

This document sits above the three companion notes. `docs/research/gfm_landscape_2026.md` establishes which models exist and which are usable; `docs/research/prior_gfm_use_eurocropsml.md` and `docs/research/prior_gfm_use_cropharvest.md` establish who has used each dataset. This document addresses a different question: what the thesis may legitimately claim as new, and what must be true for those claims to survive examination. It also supplies the justification and the citations for the methodological decisions that the Phase 1 protocol (`docs/phase1/protocol.md`) implements.

Every source below was consulted in September 2026 and is cited with author, year and a resolvable identifier. Claims that could not be traced to a primary source are collected in section 6 rather than being smoothed over.

## 1. Executive summary

### 1.1 Recommended methodological decisions

| # | Decision | One-line justification |
|---|---|---|
| **N1** | Replace the pairwise intersection of top classes with a **single fixed shared taxonomy**, defined once at HCAT depth three (the six-digit crop-group level) and applied identically to all three countries and all nine directed transfer settings. | A label space whose size changes with the country pair changes the chance level with it, so Macro-F1 ceases to be comparable across settings and the sentence "transfer to Portugal is harder" becomes untestable. |
| **N2** | Do **not delete** target classes absent from the source. Map them to a single `other` class and report both a closed-set Macro-F1 over the shared classes and an **open-set Macro-F1** that includes `other`, with abstention driven by the calibrated confidence produced in Phase 2. | Deleting them makes the cross-country test systematically easier than deployment, in which a Portuguese vineyard is present whether or not Estonia has one. This is also the precedent set by Nyborg et al. (2022) and Chang et al. (2025). |
| **N3** | Report **hierarchical precision, recall and F** over the HCAT tree as a third metric, so that predicting "cereal" for winter barley is not scored identically to predicting "permanent grassland". | The national nomenclatures differ in depth, not only in content; flat metrics discard that structure, and HCAT supplies the tree for free. |
| **N4** | Decompose the cross-country gap into **prior shift, phenological shift and residual representation failure**, and require the three components to reconstruct the observed gap. | Three published results (Adjei, 2026; Nyborg et al., 2022; Reuss et al., 2026) each attribute cross-region failure to a different one of these; a thesis that reports only the total gap cannot adjudicate between them. |
| **N5** | Equalise the **training set size** between the spatially blocked track and the official random-split track before naming their difference a spatial leakage gap; add kNNDM as a block-size-free sensitivity check; state the estimand explicitly. | Blocking removes training parcels as well as leakage, so an unadjusted gap conflates leakage with a sample-size effect, which is the standard rebuttal to block cross-validation. |
| **N6** | Keep the paired non-inferiority criterion for "matching the baseline", but add a **per-draw pass rate**, an evidence-based margin, a bracketed reporting of K\*, a horizontally read **label-saving ratio**, and multiplicity control across the many cells in which K\* is declared. | Non-inferiority is the right family of test; the current specification is under-powered against the multiplicity that five models by three heads by three countries creates. |
| **N7** | Adopt a **five-tier baseline set**: gradient-boosted trees and random forest on monthly composites and on TIMESAT phenometrics; **MiniRocket plus ridge**; an **untrained random-feature encoder control**; a supervised sequence model trained from scratch at every budget; and, optionally, TabPFN on the flattened features. | The label-efficiency claim is only as strong as the baseline it beats, and the dataset's own benchmark already reports from-scratch training beating every transfer method at five shots. |
| **N8** | Add a **spatially explicit layer**: maps of prediction, predictive entropy and error; a spatial analysis of residuals against parcel size, region, elevation and soil class; and an **area-of-applicability** analysis computed in GFM embedding space. | This is the single largest omission in the current design and the one an ITC examiner is most likely to raise. |
| **N9** | Add **temperature scaling** as a calibration arm, report Brier score, negative log-likelihood and classwise ECE alongside ECE, and state in advance that head-level Monte Carlo dropout over a frozen encoder measures head uncertainty only. | Lehmann et al. (2026) show temperature scaling is the reference method and that it fails under shift; omitting it leaves an obvious hole, and the frozen design imposes a structural limit on what the uncertainty estimate can mean. |
| **N10** | Phase 3 requires a **no-explanation control arm** and an **entailment-based traceability metric** with a human-rated calibration subset, not an unvalidated model-as-judge rubric. | Without the control, the contribution of the XAI grounding is illustrated rather than measured, and RQ3 is not answerable as posed. |

### 1.2 Surviving novelty claims, in order of defensibility

1. **A three-way decomposition of the cross-country transfer gap** for frozen geospatial foundation model features into class prior shift, phenological shift and residual representation failure. *Strong.*
2. **A quantified spatial leakage gap** on a published few-shot crop benchmark, and its interaction with the label budget. *Strong.*
3. **A frozen-encoder, per-parcel, few-shot, directed cross-country benchmark** of the 2025 to 2026 model generation on EuroCropsML, under one fixed protocol with a stated statistical criterion for a match. *Moderate to strong, once the framing is narrowed.*
4. **Backbone-tier per-band and per-date attribution on pixel-time-series foundation models** for crop classification, with faithfulness measured rather than assumed, set against an agronomic baseline. *Moderate to strong.*
5. **Calibration of frozen foundation model crop classifiers under geographic shift**, coupled to label-shift correction as its consumer. *Moderate.*
6. **An explanation-grounded language model reporting layer with an attribution-based traceability metric and a control arm.** *Moderate, and wholly contingent on the metric.*
7. **Binding embedding dimensions to agronomic concepts through linear probes.** *Weak. Killed as a standalone claim.*

### 1.3 Claims killed

- **"An agriculture-focused, few-shot, cross-region GFM benchmark does not exist."** This sentence is false as of 2026 and must be removed from the proposal. Nedungadi et al. (2026) benchmark foundation models across seven agricultural datasets; Shang et al. (2026) benchmark three across four United States states with region-disjoint splits; Chang et al. (2025) evaluate pretrained encoders for crop mapping across five continents under in-distribution and out-of-distribution splits with label budgets as small as ten images. What remains defensible is the narrower conjunction stated as claim 3 above.
- **"Binding GFM embedding dimensions to agronomic concepts through linear probes is new."** Rahman (2026) regresses AlphaEarth's sixty-four dimensions onto twenty-six environmental variables over 12.1 million samples, validates under spatial block cross-validation and publishes a dimension dictionary; Jing et al. (2026) reconstruct AlphaEarth dimensions from sparse Sentinel-1 and Sentinel-2 temporal proxies by Lasso regression and use the result for few-shot and cross-year crop classification; Benavides-Martinez et al. (2026) rank and progressively ablate the same dimensions. The instrument remains necessary to the thesis, but it is an instrument, not a contribution.
- **"No published XAI work exists on GFM embeddings."** The proposal's Gap 2 as currently worded overstates the position and should be rewritten around the backbone tier and around the joint treatment with uncertainty, which is where the residual gap actually lies.

## 2. Task 1: what has been published on cross-region generalisation for crop classification

### 2.1 The baseline expectation set by the non-foundation-model literature

Cross-region transfer of crop classifiers fails hard, and it has been known to fail hard for some years. This matters for the thesis because it fixes the reference against which a foundation model result must be read: a frozen encoder that loses twenty points across a border has not necessarily done badly, because the classical expectation is a loss of forty or more.

The sharpest published quantification is **TimeMatch** (Nyborg, Pelletier, Lefèvre and Assent, 2022, *ISPRS Journal of Photogrammetry and Remote Sensing* 188, 301 to 313, [arXiv:2111.02682](https://arxiv.org/abs/2111.02682)). The authors release a four-region parcel-level Sentinel-2 dataset covering Austria (tile 33UVP), Denmark (32VNH) and two French regions (30TXT and 31TCJ), with fifteen major European crop classes. Their protocol is instructive on three counts. First, the label space is handled by selecting classes with at least two hundred examples in the source region and assigning the remainder to an explicit `unknown` category, rather than by deleting them. Second, the encoder is a supervised sequence model trained on the source and applied to the target without target labels. Third, the reference points are both a source-only lower bound and a target-supervised upper bound on the same label space. The headline numbers are the ones the thesis should quote as its expectation: source-trained models reach an average F1 of approximately 39 per cent on target data against approximately 82 per cent for target-supervised training, a gap of some forty-three points, with individual scenarios as extreme as 24.9 per cent against 90.6 per cent for France to Denmark. The paper's contribution is that a large part of that gap is a temporal shift in phenology rather than a spectral or structural difference, and that estimating and correcting the shift recovers roughly eleven F1 points on average.

For the tree-ensemble family, **Orynbaikyzy et al. (2022)**, *Remote Sensing* 14(6), 1493, [doi:10.3390/rs14061493](https://doi.org/10.3390/rs14061493), assess the spatial transferability of random forest crop classifiers across Germany with Sentinel-1 and Sentinel-2 inputs. Within training sites the optical and radar combination reaches a maximum F1 of 0.85, falling to 0.79 on areas not covered by training data. Notably, SAR-only models lose the least, at an average F1 loss of 0.04, which is evidence that the transferable signal is structural rather than purely spectral, and that optical models are the ones most exposed to phenological and atmospheric differences between regions. This is the most directly comparable classical reference for the thesis's raw-feature baseline, since it is a handcrafted-feature random forest evaluated on unseen geography.

A larger-scale and more recent study extends the same question across continents. *Investigation of the spatio-temporal transferability of large-scale random forest crop classification models and potential influencing factors* (2025, *International Journal of Digital Earth*, [doi:10.1080/17538947.2025.2563211](https://doi.org/10.1080/17538947.2025.2563211)) trains random forests on Landsat and Sentinel-2 time series from three United States states and applies them to six European countries. Performance varies by up to 21.6 percentage points of accuracy depending only on which state supplied the training data, which is a direct demonstration that the source region is itself a design variable and not a neutral choice. The thesis's nine directed settings exist for precisely this reason and should cite this result when defending the decision to run both directions of every pair.

The unsupervised domain adaptation literature that has grown around this problem is large and is worth citing as context rather than as method, since the thesis deliberately does not adapt the encoder. Representative entries include an unsupervised domain adaptation method for spatially and temporally transferable crop mapping (*ISPRS Journal of Photogrammetry and Remote Sensing*, 2023, [S0924271623000850](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S0924271623000850)), a source-free variant for cross-regional and cross-time crop mapping (*Remote Sensing of Environment*, 2024, [S0034425724004115](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S0034425724004115)) and a class-aware framework for cross-continental crop classification (2025, [doi:10.3390/rs17223762](https://doi.org/10.3390/rs17223762)). Their shared premise is that the representation must be adapted for transfer to work at all. A frozen-encoder thesis is testing whether foundation model pretraining removes that necessity, and should say so explicitly, because that is the scientific question the frozen design actually poses.

### 2.2 Foundation-model-specific cross-region evaluation

**Chang, Stewart, Bastani, Wolters, Kannan, Huber, Wang and Banerjee (2025), *On the generalizability of foundation models for crop type mapping*, IGARSS 2025, [arXiv:2409.09451](https://arxiv.org/abs/2409.09451).** This is the closest published antecedent to the thesis's Phase 1 cross-country arm, and it must be characterised precisely, because what it leaves open is the space the thesis occupies.

Protocol, established from the paper itself:

- **Datasets.** Five crop datasets on five continents: the United States Cropland Data Layer, EuroCrops for Europe, the Northeastern China Crop Map, South Africa Crop Type, and South America Soybean. Dataset sizes are normalised so that the comparison is not a comparison of dataset scale.
- **Label space.** All national schemes are collapsed into six categories: no data, maize, soybean, rice, wheat and other known. The stated rationale is that these are the four most valuable crops and account for the bulk of global cereal production.
- **Encoder state.** The ResNet-50 encoder is **frozen** and a U-Net decoder is **fine-tuned** on top of it. This is not a frozen-features protocol in the sense the thesis uses; a fine-tuned decoder carries a large parameter budget of its own.
- **Task shape.** Semantic segmentation of 256 by 256 pixel patches at 10 m resolution, not per-parcel classification.
- **Label budget.** Ten, one hundred and nine hundred labelled samples per region.
- **Splits.** In-distribution means training and testing in the same region; out-of-distribution means training on data from other regions and testing on a region not seen during development.
- **Models.** Three sets of pretrained weights: SSL4EO-S12, SatlasPretrain and ImageNet.
- **Conclusions.** Weights designed explicitly for Sentinel-2, namely SSL4EO-S12, outperform general-purpose weights by 3 to 27 per cent across study regions; one hundred labelled samples suffice for high overall accuracy but nine hundred are required for high average accuracy because of class imbalance.

What this leaves open, and therefore what the thesis can occupy:

1. **Model generation.** SSL4EO-S12 and SatlasPretrain are pretrained weight sets from 2022 and 2023. None of TerraMind, THOR, AlphaEarth, TESSERA, Galileo, Presto or OlmoEarth appears.
2. **Encoder state.** A fine-tuned U-Net decoder is not the frozen-features regime under which precomputed-embedding products such as AlphaEarth must be used, and it is not the regime in which the label-efficiency question is sharpest.
3. **Granularity of the label space.** Four cereals plus an `other` bucket is a coarse task. The thesis's fifteen to twenty class per-country scheme, and its HCAT-derived shared taxonomy, pose a materially harder problem, and the gap between the two is itself a result worth reporting.
4. **Temporal representation.** The task is patch segmentation; the per-parcel phenological time series that distinguishes winter from spring cereals is not the unit of analysis.
5. **Transparency.** No explainability, no uncertainty, no calibration.
6. **Statistics.** Performance is compared by inspection, not by a stated criterion with an error rate.

**Shang, Das and Eldawy (2026), *Benchmarking geospatial foundation models for agriculture applications*, [arXiv:2606.29664](https://arxiv.org/abs/2606.29664).** Prithvi, SpectralGPT and SatMAE on Sentinel-2 Level-2A from the 2024 season across Iowa, North Carolina, California and Minnesota, with labels from the 2024 USDA Cropland Data Layer mapped to thirteen classes (seven crops and six land-cover categories). The methodologically important choice is that each state is divided into three non-overlapping contiguous regions of 800 to 1300 square kilometres, with train, validation and test each assigned to a separate geography, so that evaluation reflects transfer to unseen land rather than spatial overlap. All three models are fully fine-tuned. The finding is severe geographic degradation: SpectralGPT falls from 25.95 crop mIoU in North Carolina to 7.45 in California, minority crops such as winter wheat and sorghum are near zero almost everywhere, and the authors conclude that "geography matters as much as architecture" and that "strong numbers on one dataset do not promise the same elsewhere". This is a within-country region-disjoint design, not a cross-country one, and it is fine-tuned rather than frozen, but it is the strongest recent statement of the phenomenon and the thesis should cite it when motivating the cross-country arm.

**Adjei (2026), *Do foundation model embeddings improve cross-country crop yield generalisation? A leave-one-country-out evaluation in sub-Saharan Africa*, [arXiv:2605.08113](https://arxiv.org/abs/2605.08113).** This is the closest published analogue to the thesis's experimental logic, transposed from classification to regression. Prithvi-EO-1.0-100M and an ImageNet ViT-Base are used as **frozen** embedding extractors, against a spectral-feature baseline, with ridge regression, random forest and XGBoost heads; the protocol is strict leave-one-country-out over Kenya, Malawi, Nigeria, Rwanda and Tanzania. Within-country R squared reaches 0.30 while leave-one-country-out R squared is universally negative, between minus 0.09 and minus 0.03, matching or underperforming simple spectral indices. The author's conclusion is the one the thesis must engage with directly: the bottleneck is country-level distribution shift rather than feature representation inadequacy. This is exactly the confound that problem 2 in section 3 addresses, and it is the reason the decomposition is claim 1 in the novelty ranking rather than a methodological footnote.

**Nedungadi, Xiong, Rußwurm and Athanasiadis (2026), *Foundation models meet agriculture: challenges beyond pretraining*, [arXiv:2608.30392](https://arxiv.org/abs/2608.30392).** Seven agricultural datasets spanning yield prediction, phenology estimation and crop classification; two Earth observation foundation models, one tabular foundation model and conventional supervised baselines. The framing is blunt: early attempts to deploy foundation models for agricultural applications have yielded surprisingly poor results, attributed to a modality gap and to structural heterogeneity that destabilises model rankings, with a tabular foundation model handling that heterogeneity more naturally than vision-based ones. The companion note on CropHarvest records the specific numbers that matter: on CropHarvest Kenya a plain supervised Transformer reaches 0.89 F1 against frozen Galileo's 0.70, and on BreizhCrops 0.60 against frozen CropFM's 0.38. EuroCrops and EuroCropsML are explicitly excluded. This paper is simultaneously the strongest threat to the thesis's premise and the strongest justification for its baseline set.

**Lauber, Turkoglu, Ledain and Aasen (2026), SwissCrop25, [arXiv:2608.09497](https://arxiv.org/abs/2608.09497)**, reports frozen Galileo-Nano at 14.1 per cent mIoU against 30.4 per cent fine-tuned and 48.1 per cent for the supervised specialist TSViT on a Swiss national benchmark. Again this is not a cross-country study, but it bounds the expectation for a frozen encoder on a national crop task and it is a further reason the thesis must include a from-scratch supervised arm.

**Multi-domain benchmarks and why they do not close the gap.** PANGAEA (Marsocci et al., 2024, [arXiv:2412.04204](https://arxiv.org/abs/2412.04204)) introduces a limited-label setting at ten per cent of the training labels and reports that with limited labels the supervised baselines, especially UNet, outperform most of the models, and more generally that foundation models do not consistently outperform supervised models. It is multi-domain and has no cross-region protocol. GEO-Bench-2 (Simumba, Lehmann, Fraccaro and colleagues, 2025 to 2026, [arXiv:2511.15658](https://arxiv.org/abs/2511.15658)) was inspected directly for this document: it contains nineteen datasets in nine capability groups, includes PASTIS-R as its crop task, and its protocol is explicitly end-to-end fine-tuning with the pretrained backbone unfrozen, accompanied by the observation that full fine-tuning consistently outperformed the frozen-backbone approach. It has **no few-shot or label-efficiency protocol** and **no geographic out-of-distribution split**; its own limitations section concedes that dataset coverage remains biased towards Europe and North America. Its statistical machinery is nonetheless worth borrowing: five repetitions per configuration, a stratified bootstrap over the normalised repeats with one hundred resamples, and an interquartile mean aggregation to discard outliers.

### 2.3 EuroCrops and EuroCropsML specifically

The companion note `prior_gfm_use_eurocropsml.md` establishes the central fact, which this document endorses: **no published or preprint work has evaluated a released geospatial foundation model on EuroCropsML.** The dataset's own benchmark work is therefore the relevant comparison.

Reuss et al. (2025, *Scientific Data*, [doi:10.1038/s41597-025-04952-7](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41597-025-04952-7)) describe EuroCropsML as the first dataset designed to benchmark transnational few-shot crop type classification algorithms, with 706,683 parcels and 176 classes over Estonia, Latvia and Portugal. Reuss et al. (2025b, [arXiv:2504.11022](https://arxiv.org/abs/2504.11022), published in the *ISPRS Open Journal of Photogrammetry and Remote Sensing*) supply the benchmark itself: a Transformer encoder with a linear head, K in {1, 5, 10, 20, 100, 200, 500}, five seeds, accuracy and Cohen's kappa, and two transfer scenarios, Latvia to Estonia and Latvia plus Portugal to Estonia, with 103 to 127 classes per region. Three of their findings constrain the thesis directly.

- **Training from scratch on the target beats every transfer method at five shots** (0.331 against 0.203 for supervised transfer and 0.259 for MAML) and remains within a few points at twenty and one hundred shots. The bar that a frozen foundation model must clear is not supervised transfer; it is a Transformer trained from scratch on K parcels.
- **Adding Portugal to the pre-training pool degrades transfer to Estonia.** Supervised transfer falls from 0.365 to 0.292 at twenty shots when Portugal is added to Latvia as a source. This is the clearest existing evidence that source composition matters and is the direct antecedent of the thesis's three many-to-one settings.
- **The label space is left at full national depth**, 103 to 127 classes, with no intersection taken. The thesis's restriction to fifteen to twenty classes per country is therefore a departure that must be defended rather than assumed, and the Macro-F1 it reports is not comparable line by line with the published accuracies.

Two follow-ups by the same group bear directly on problem 2 below. *Mind the gap: bridging prior shift in realistic few-shot crop-type classification* ([arXiv:2511.16218](https://arxiv.org/abs/2511.16218)) and *DirPA: addressing prior shift in imbalanced few-shot crop-type classification* ([arXiv:2603.12905](https://arxiv.org/abs/2603.12905)) treat class prior shift as the central obstacle in exactly this dataset. That the dataset authors have devoted two papers to prior shift is strong evidence that a thesis reporting only an aggregate transfer score on the same data will be asked, in the defence, whether it has measured priors or representations.

The parent dataset has also moved. EuroCrops v2.0 (*Earth System Science Data* 18, 4075, 2026, [essd.copernicus.org/articles/18/4075/2026](https://essd.copernicus.org/articles/18/4075/2026)) is multi-annual and linked to European Union survey, statistical and Earth observation products, and DirPA reports results on an EuroCropsML 2.0 covering Austria, Belgium and further regions. Whether to migrate is an open question for the supervisors; it is flagged here because a multi-annual release would make the temporal-transfer arm discussed in section 2.4 available at no additional data cost.

Finally, the hierarchical crop and agriculture taxonomy that EuroCrops introduced is the instrument on which the recommendation in section 3.1 depends. HCAT structures roughly four hundred European crop classes into a six-level tree encoded as a ten-digit code, in which the leading "33" is fixed and each subsequent pair of digits denotes one level, so that 3301010101 (winter common soft wheat) rolls up to 3301010100 (common soft wheat), then to 3301010000 (cereal) and then to 3301000000 (arable crops) (Schneider et al., 2023, *Scientific Data*, [doi:10.1038/s41597-023-02517-0](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41597-023-02517-0); updated taxonomy in *npj Sustainable Agriculture*, 2024, [doi:10.1038/s44264-024-00037-x](https://www.nature.com/articles/s44264-024-00037-x)). Coarsening is therefore an exact string truncation, is reversible, and is documentable in a single table.

### 2.4 Temporal transfer, where it informs the spatial design

Temporal transfer is the methodological sibling of spatial transfer and two results from it carry over.

The first is that the mechanism is often the same. TimeMatch identifies the dominant cross-region failure as a **shift in the timing of phenology**, and the same shift arises between years in a single region because of weather. This is why the thesis should treat a phenological alignment term as a named component of the transfer gap rather than as an unexplained residual: the instrument that removes a cross-year gap is the instrument that removes part of a cross-country gap.

The second is that foundation model embeddings have already been tested under temporal transfer for crops, with mixed results. Lisaius, Keshav, Blake and Atzberger (2026, [arXiv:2601.16900](https://arxiv.org/abs/2601.16900)) report TESSERA outperforming competing approaches by 28 per cent in temporal transfer in the Groundnut Basin of Senegal, while Jing et al. (2026, *Big Earth Data*, [doi:10.1080/20964471.2026.2691477](https://doi.org/10.1080/20964471.2026.2691477)) find that AlphaEarth dimensions reconstructed from sparse Sentinel-1 and Sentinel-2 proxies transfer across years at least as well as the originals, on maize and soybean in the United States Corn Belt at budgets of ten to two hundred labelled samples.

EuroCropsML version 1 is single-year (2021), so a temporal arm is not available without migrating to a multi-annual release. The recommendation is therefore modest: **do not add a temporal transfer experiment**, but do borrow its instrument, temporal shift estimation, as a diagnostic inside the spatial experiment, and state in the discussion that the single-year design means the thesis cannot separate a country effect from a country-by-year interaction. That limitation is real and should be declared rather than left for an examiner to find.

## 3. Task 2: the methodological problems that cross-region transfer raises

### 3.1 Problem 1: disjoint label spaces

#### The problem, stated precisely

Estonia, Latvia and Portugal declare different crops under different national nomenclatures, harmonised into HCAT but not made identical by it. The proposal and the Phase 1 protocol answer this by taking, for each ordered pair of countries, the intersection of the two countries' top-N class sets, and by deleting from both partitions every parcel whose class falls outside that intersection. The protocol already records that this yields approximately nine classes for the Estonia and Latvia pairs at full HCAT depth and approximately one for the Portugal pairs, rising to approximately three when HCAT is truncated to six digits.

Three distinct objections arise, and they are not equally serious.

**Objection 1: the intersection is a biased sample of the difficulty distribution.** Intersecting top-N lists retains the classes that are frequent in both countries. In this study area those are permanent grassland, the winter and spring cereals, rapeseed and fodder. These are precisely the classes with the most training data, the most distinct phenological signatures and, in the Baltic case, the largest and most homogeneous parcels. The classes discarded are the regionally distinctive ones, which in Portugal means vineyards, olive groves, rice, cork oak and the Mediterranean permanent crops, and which in the Baltics means the minor legumes and the specialist fodder mixtures. A protocol that systematically removes the regionally distinctive classes from a test of regional generalisation is measuring something narrower than it claims.

**Objection 2, which is the serious one: deletion removes the operational failure mode.** In deployment, a classifier trained in Estonia and applied in Portugal meets olive groves whether or not Estonia has any. Under the intersection protocol those parcels are deleted from the target test set, so the model is never charged for confidently assigning them to a Baltic class. The score obtained is therefore an upper bound on deployment performance, and its bias is in the direction that flatters the foundation models, because a strong representation and a weak one are equally unpenalised for a mistake they are never asked to make. This is not a subtle bias: in Portugal the deleted fraction of the test set would be the majority of it.

**Objection 3: a pairwise-varying label space destroys comparability across settings.** If Estonia to Latvia is scored over nine classes and Estonia to Portugal over three, the two Macro-F1 values sit on different scales with different chance levels, roughly 0.11 against 0.33 for a uniform random classifier. The sentence "transfer to Portugal is harder than transfer to Latvia" is then not testable from the numbers, and the Phase 1 protocol is already forced to warn that the two must never be placed on the same axis. A design that forbids its own primary comparison should be changed rather than annotated.

#### How the field handles it

There are four families of answer in the literature, and the field has converged on the first two.

1. **A fixed shared taxonomy, defined once.** Chang et al. (2025) collapse five continental schemes into maize, soybean, rice, wheat and `other`. Every dataset is scored on the same six categories, so the cross-continental comparison is well defined. The cost is coarseness; the benefit is that one number means the same thing everywhere.
2. **A shared taxonomy plus an explicit unknown bucket.** Nyborg et al. (2022) retain fifteen shared classes, require at least two hundred source examples per class, and assign everything else to `unknown` rather than deleting it. The unknown class is present in the target and the model is accountable for it.
3. **Hierarchical labels and hierarchical scoring.** Turkoglu et al. (2021, [arXiv:2102.08820](https://arxiv.org/abs/2102.08820)) exploit the fact that agricultural experts already work with a tree, predict at three levels of granularity simultaneously, and show that coarse-level supervision materially improves fine-level performance on rare classes. Hierarchical precision, recall and F measures are standard in the hierarchical classification literature (Kiritchenko et al., 2006; Silla and Freitas, 2011) and give partial credit for a prediction that is correct at an ancestor node.
4. **Open-set recognition.** The target contains classes the source never saw, and the classifier is equipped with a reject option. OpenMax (Bendale and Boult, 2016) is the canonical deep formulation and open-set domain adaptation (Saito et al., 2018) the transfer formulation. Applications to crop mapping exist but are sparse and none is standard.

#### Recommendation

**Adopt a single fixed shared taxonomy with an explicit `other` class, and report three metrics.** Concretely:

1. **Define one shared label space for the whole cross-country experiment, before any result is seen.** Take the union of the three countries' eligible classes, truncate every HCAT code to a single declared depth, and keep every truncated class that is eligible in at least two of the three countries. The recommended depth is six digits, the crop-group level, because the Phase 1 protocol has already established empirically that it is the shallowest level at which Portugal shares anything with the Baltics and that deeper truncation collapses the Baltic distinctions as well. The resulting taxonomy is used **identically in all nine directed settings**, so every Macro-F1 in the transfer experiment has the same chance level and the same denominator.
2. **Retain non-shared target parcels as `other`.** Any target parcel whose class is eligible in the target but not in the shared space is mapped to a single additional class. The source-trained head cannot predict `other` directly; it predicts `other` by abstaining, using a confidence threshold fixed on source validation data. Report the **closed-set Macro-F1** over the shared classes, computed on shared-class parcels only, which is comparable with prior work and with the intersection protocol, and the **open-set Macro-F1** including `other`, which is the operational number. The difference between the two is a directly interpretable quantity: the cost of meeting crops the source country does not grow.
3. **Report hierarchical F as a third metric** over the full HCAT tree at native depth. This costs nothing, because the tree is already in the data, and it distinguishes a model that confuses winter and spring barley from one that confuses barley with grassland. It is also the metric that makes the Portugal settings interpretable at all, because a Portuguese prediction that is correct at the crop-group level but wrong at the species level is a materially better outcome than a random one.
4. **Keep the pairwise intersection as a secondary comparability track**, reported in an appendix, so that the numbers can be placed beside Reuss et al. and beside the protocol as originally specified. Demote it from the headline.

Three further consequences should be written into the methods chapter rather than discovered later. First, the shared taxonomy makes the in-country and cross-country experiments commensurable, so the label-saving ratio that the Phase 1 protocol defines for transfer curves can also be computed against the in-country curve. Second, the abstention threshold is supplied by Phase 2, which converts the uncertainty work from an add-on into a dependency of Phase 1, and that dependency is worth stating explicitly as an argument for the thesis's integrated design. Third, the `other` class is by construction heterogeneous, so its per-class F1 should be reported but never averaged into a claim about a specific crop.

**Against open-set recognition as a headline.** A full open-set treatment, with reciprocal points, extreme-value calibration or adversarial reconstruction, would be a second thesis. The abstention-plus-`other` design above captures the operational failure mode at the cost of one threshold, and it degrades gracefully: if abstention proves useless, the closed-set number is still reported and the finding is that calibrated confidence does not separate unseen crops from seen ones, which is a genuine and publishable negative result about the representations.

### 3.2 Problem 2: class prior shift against representation failure

#### The problem, stated precisely

A head trained on Estonian embeddings and applied in Portugal may lose thirty points of Macro-F1 for at least four distinct reasons, which have different implications and different remedies:

1. **Prior shift.** The marginal class distribution differs, so a head whose decision boundaries were placed under Estonian priors is systematically miscalibrated in Portugal even if the class-conditional feature distributions are identical.
2. **Phenological shift.** The class-conditional distributions differ only by a translation in time, because the Portuguese crop calendar is displaced relative to the Baltic one. The representation is perfectly adequate; the inputs are misaligned.
3. **Representation failure.** The class-conditional distributions differ in shape, so the frozen embedding does not place the same crop in the same region of embedding space in both countries. This is the finding that would actually bear on the value of foundation model pretraining.
4. **Label-space and label-quality artefacts.** The two national nomenclatures mean subtly different things by the same HCAT code, or the declaration error rate differs between the two administrations.

Reporting a single aggregate transfer score makes these indistinguishable, and the literature is currently split on which dominates. Adjei (2026) concludes for country-level distribution shift; Nyborg et al. (2022) demonstrate that a large share is temporal misalignment; Reuss et al. (2026) devote two papers to prior shift in this exact dataset; Shang et al. (2026) attribute failure to representation. The thesis is therefore entering a live disagreement, which is an opportunity, not a hazard, provided that it measures rather than asserts.

#### How the field handles it

- **Prior correction by expectation maximisation.** Saerens, Latinne and Decaestecker (2002, *Neural Computation* 14(1), 21 to 41) give the standard procedure for adjusting a classifier's posteriors to new priors, estimating the target prior by expectation maximisation from unlabelled target data. This is the method the Phase 1 protocol already specifies.
- **Black-box shift estimation.** Lipton, Wang and Smola (2018, ICML, [arXiv:1802.03916](https://arxiv.org/abs/1802.03916)) estimate the target prior from the source confusion matrix. BBSE is consistent with interpretable error bounds and, critically, **does not require the classifier to be calibrated**, only that its confusion matrix be invertible.
- **The calibration dependency.** Alexandari, Kundaje and Shrikumar (2020, ICML, [arXiv:1901.06852](https://arxiv.org/abs/1901.06852)) and Garg et al. (2020, NeurIPS, [arXiv:2003.07554](https://arxiv.org/abs/2003.07554)) establish that maximum-likelihood label shift adaptation, of which the Saerens procedure is an instance, dominates BBSE empirically **but only when the predictor is well calibrated**, and that bias-corrected temperature scaling is the calibration that makes it work. This is the single most useful methodological link this document can offer the thesis: the calibration work of RQ2.2 is a precondition for the prior-shift decomposition of Phase 1, not a parallel activity.
- **Balanced or prior-matched evaluation.** Resampling the target test set to match the source priors removes the prior term by construction, at the cost of variance and of discarding data.
- **Per-class rather than aggregate reporting.** A uniform per-class fall indicates a representation problem; a fall concentrated in the classes whose prior moves most indicates a prior problem. This is the cheapest diagnostic available and should always be reported.

#### Recommendation

**Report an additive four-term decomposition of the transfer gap, and require the terms to reconstruct the observed gap.** Define, on the shared label space and the fixed target test set:

```
G_total = MacroF1( target-only, POOL )  -  MacroF1( source-trained, zero-shot on target )
```

and decompose it as

```
G_total  =  G_spatial  +  G_prior  +  G_phenology  +  G_representation
```

where each term is measured, not assumed:

1. **`G_spatial`, the irreducible within-country blocked gap.** The difference between a target-only head evaluated on held-out blocks of its own country and the same head evaluated on its own training blocks. This bounds the part of any cross-country gap that is simply the cost of predicting somewhere new, and it is available for free from the in-country experiment. Without this term the cross-country gap is inflated by a quantity that has nothing to do with the border.
2. **`G_prior`.** The improvement obtained by correcting the source-trained posteriors for the target prior. Use the Saerens expectation-maximisation procedure on the **calibrated** head as primary, and BBSE as a calibration-free cross-check; if the two disagree substantially, the calibration is the suspect and that is itself reportable. Cross-validate the result against a prior-matched target subsample, which should recover a similar quantity by a different route.
3. **`G_phenology`.** The further improvement obtained by re-indexing the target time series by an estimated temporal offset before extracting embeddings, following the shift-estimation idea of Nyborg et al. (2022): estimate the offset that maximises the source-trained model's confidence on unlabelled target data, or derive it independently from accumulated growing degree days or from the NDVI peak day-of-year that the TIMESAT baseline already computes. This term is cheap for the pixel-time-series models, because it is a re-indexing of the input rather than a retraining, and it is the term the current protocol lacks entirely.
4. **`G_representation`.** The residual. This is the quantity that actually answers whether foundation model pretraining produces a geographically invariant crop representation, and it is the number the thesis should put in its abstract.

Four supporting instruments should accompany the decomposition, three of which the Phase 1 protocol already specifies and should be retained:

- **Per-class F1 differences** against the target-only reference at the same budget, always reported.
- **Balanced accuracy alongside Macro-F1**, since the two respond differently to prior shift.
- **A prior-only oracle floor**: a featureless classifier that draws from the target prior. Any transfer score at or below this floor indicates that the representation is contributing nothing, and reporting it prevents a positive-sounding Macro-F1 over a skewed label space from being over-interpreted.
- **Covariates of the gap across the nine settings**: the Jensen-Shannon divergence between source and target priors over the shared space, and the mean absolute shift in NDVI peak day-of-year per class. Regressing `G_total` on both, across nine settings, gives a small but genuine quantitative result about what predicts transfer difficulty, and it is the natural figure for the cross-country chapter.

A final caution. Nine settings is a small sample for a regression, and the settings are not independent, since each country appears in six of them. The regression should be presented as descriptive, with the settings labelled on the plot, and no p-value should be attached to it.

### 3.3 Problem 3: spatial autocorrelation and leakage

The Phase 1 protocol specifies the implementation. This section supplies the justification, the citations and three corrections.

#### What current practice actually is

Roberts et al. (2017, *Ecography* 40(8), 913 to 929, [doi:10.1111/ecog.02881](https://doi.org/10.1111/ecog.02881)) remains the standard reference for the general argument that structured data require structured cross-validation, and for the block design itself. In remote sensing specifically, Karasiak et al. (2022, *Machine Learning*) demonstrate that spatial dependence between training and test sets inflates land-cover classification accuracy and advocate spatial leave-one-out designs, and Ploton et al. (2020, *Nature Communications*) show for a mapping application that random cross-validation produces strongly overoptimistic accuracy while spatial cross-validation does not. Valavi et al. (2019) provide the widely used `blockCV` implementation, including the convention of choosing a block size from the range of spatial autocorrelation.

Recent crop-specific practice has adopted region-disjoint splits as a matter of course. Shang et al. (2026) assign train, validation and test to separate contiguous regions within each state precisely so that evaluation "reflects transfer to unseen geographic areas rather than spatial overlap"; Adjei (2026) contrasts leave-one-country-out with "standard random cross-validation that leaks country-specific patterns into both training and test sets". The thesis is therefore aligned with current practice, and the more interesting contribution is to **measure** the difference rather than merely to avoid it.

#### Choosing the block size defensibly

The protocol estimates the block edge from a label-agreement decay curve, the categorical analogue of an empirical variogram, and takes three times the fitted decay constant as a practical range. This is a sound and defensible construction and it satisfies the requirement that the block size not be chosen by convention. Two refinements make it more defensible still.

**First, the relevant range is that of the model residuals, not of the raw labels.** Roberts et al. (2017) are explicit that block size should reflect the autocorrelation remaining after the model has used the covariates, because a model that explains the spatial pattern through its features does not leak through it. A label-agreement range is the upper bound; a residual-based range is the operative one. The recommendation is to compute both, report the label-based range as the primary because it is model-independent and can be fixed before any model exists, and compute a residual-based range once one model per family exists, as a sensitivity check. If the residual range is much shorter, the blocked protocol is conservative, which is the safe direction and should be stated.

**Second, a block-size-free alternative exists and should be run as a check.** Milà, Mateu, Pebesma and Meyer (2022, *Methods in Ecology and Evolution*, [doi:10.1111/2041-210X.13851](https://besjournals.onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/abs/10.1111/2041-210X.13851)) propose nearest-neighbour distance matching leave-one-out cross-validation, and Linnenbrink et al. (2024, *Geoscientific Model Development* 17, 5897, [gmd.copernicus.org/articles/17/5897/2024](https://gmd.copernicus.org/articles/17/5897/2024)) give the k-fold version, kNNDM. The principle removes the arbitrariness entirely: folds are constructed so that the distribution of nearest-neighbour distances from test points to training points matches the distribution of distances from the intended prediction locations to the training points. The thesis is unusually well placed to use this, because its prediction domain is explicit and finite: the rest of the country in the in-country experiment, and the target country in the transfer experiment. Running kNNDM alongside the fixed grid, at a reduced budget grid, converts an assumption into a measurement and costs very little.

#### The criticisms, and how to defuse them

Wadoux, Heuvelink, de Bruin and Brus (2021, *Ecological Modelling* 457, 109692, [S0304380021002489](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S0304380021002489)) argue that spatial cross-validation has no theoretical underpinning for the estimation of map accuracy and should not be used for that purpose, and that valid, design-unbiased accuracy assessment requires probability sampling and design-based inference. A related and equally important criticism is that spatial cross-validation can be **pessimistically biased**, because holding out a block removes training data near the test locations, so the blocked estimate confounds spatial extrapolation with a reduction in effective training density; de Bruin et al. (2022) find that the proposed remedies are not universal and, depending on the sampling design, over- or underestimate true map accuracy.

The defence is to state the estimand. The thesis is **not** estimating the accuracy of a map over a defined area; it is estimating the expected performance of a model applied to fields it has not seen, in places it has not been trained. For that prediction-oriented estimand, spatial separation is the correct design and random splitting is not, and this is the position taken by Roberts et al. (2017), by Karasiak et al. (2022) and by the recent prediction-domain adaptive evaluation literature ([arXiv:2605.13689](https://arxiv.org/abs/2605.13689)). One paragraph in the methods chapter making this distinction, and citing Wadoux et al. explicitly rather than ignoring them, removes the objection. If the thesis ever does claim a map product, then design-based assessment applies and is discussed in section 5.6.

**The correction the Phase 1 protocol needs.** Decision D4 defines a "spatial leakage gap" as the difference in Macro-F1 between the blocked track and the official random-split track on the shared Latvia to Estonia cell. As specified, that difference contains two things: leakage, and the fact that the blocked track has fewer training parcels after the one-kilometre buffer removes between one and thirteen per cent of the pool. **Equalise the training set size before taking the difference**, by subsampling the random-split training pool to the size of the buffered blocked pool at every budget. At small K this is automatic, since the support set is exactly K per class in both tracks, so the correction matters only at the larger budgets and at POOL, but it is exactly at POOL that the gap will be quoted. Without this control the headline number is not a leakage estimate, and a careful examiner will say so.

Two further points worth writing down. Leakage is expected to be **largest at small K**, because a single leaked neighbour is a large fraction of a five-label support set, so the gap should be reported as a function of K rather than as a scalar; that functional form is the genuinely novel part of the result. And the buffer width sensitivity that the protocol already specifies, at zero, one and two kilometres, is the right instrument for showing that the gap is not an artefact of one arbitrary choice.

### 3.4 Problem 4: what "matching the baseline" means

#### The problem, stated precisely

RQ1 asks how few labelled samples per class a geospatial foundation model needs in order to match a raw-feature baseline. Three things must be fixed before that question has an answer: what "match" means, against which baseline score, and with what evidential standard.

The failure mode to avoid is the common one in the label-efficiency literature, in which two learning curves are plotted with shaded confidence bands and a crossing point is declared where the bands begin to overlap. Overlap of two intervals is not a test. It has no stated error rate, and the relationship between interval overlap and a formal test depends on the relative widths of the two intervals, so the procedure is conservative by an amount that varies from cell to cell (Schenker and Gentleman, 2001). More fundamentally, "the two are the same" is not the alternative hypothesis of a standard significance test; failing to reject a difference is not evidence of equivalence. Establishing equivalence requires the equivalence-testing family: two one-sided tests (Schuirmann, 1987), or a one-sided non-inferiority test when only one direction matters, with a margin declared in advance (Lakens, 2017).

A second failure mode is specific to few-shot benchmarking. At K equal to five, the dominant source of variance is not the test set but the **draw**, that is, which five parcels per class the annotator happened to collect. Bouthillier et al. (2021, MLSys, [arXiv:2103.03098](https://arxiv.org/abs/2103.03098)) show that data sampling, initialisation and hyperparameter choice all materially affect machine learning benchmark conclusions and that a single trial is insufficient. A criterion that averages over draws and then tests the average answers a question no practitioner asks, because the practitioner gets one draw.

#### Recommendation

The Phase 1 protocol's decision D12, a one-sided paired non-inferiority criterion at a margin of 0.02 Macro-F1 with a block cluster bootstrap and a monotone-attainment clause, is correct in its essentials and should be kept. Five additions make it defensible under examination.

1. **Justify the margin from evidence, and fix it before results.** A margin of 0.02 Macro-F1 declared by fiat invites the question of why not 0.05. Two anchors are available. The first is label quality: parcel declarations in the Integrated Administration and Control System carry an error rate that is expected to be around two per cent and required to stay below five per cent, and empirical filtering studies of such data report comparable magnitudes (Gkounti et al., 2022, ISPRS Archives XLIII-B3, 871). No difference smaller than the label error rate is measurable in any operationally meaningful sense. The second is the observed precision of the experiment: half the median width of the test-set bootstrap interval at POOL. Recommend `delta = max(0.02, half the median POOL interval width)`, computed once on the baseline only, before any foundation model result is inspected, and recorded in the configuration.
2. **Report the per-draw pass rate alongside the pooled decision.** For each K, state in how many of the R support draws the paired criterion holds. A K\* that holds on ten of ten draws and a K\* that holds on six of ten are different findings, and the second one is arguably the more honest answer to "how few labels does a practitioner need". This costs nothing, since the per-draw predictions are already persisted.
3. **Report K\* as a bracket, never as a point.** The grid is coarse and approximately logarithmic, so the correct statement is "between 20 and 50 labels per class", not "50". The protocol already forbids interpolation; the bracket notation makes the coarseness visible in every table.
4. **Add the label-saving ratio as the primary continuous quantity.** K\* is a grid-quantised statistic and is therefore unstable near a grid boundary. The horizontal displacement between the foundation model curve and the baseline curve at a fixed Macro-F1 level, expressed as a ratio of budgets and read at several levels, is continuous, is robust to grid placement, and is the quantity the label-efficiency literature actually uses when it says a method is "eight times more label efficient". The Phase 1 protocol already defines this for transfer curves; extend it to the in-country foundation model against baseline comparison and give it a bootstrap interval.
5. **Control multiplicity.** With five models, three head families, three countries and two directions of comparison, K\* is declared in of the order of one hundred cells, each by a one-sided test at the 2.5 per cent level. Some will pass by chance. Either pre-register one headline head per model and treat the remainder as exploratory, which is the simpler option and is recommended, or control the false discovery rate across the declarations (Benjamini and Hochberg, 1995). The monotone-attainment clause reduces the problem but does not control it, because it is a within-curve constraint and multiplicity is across curves.

**On the choice of reference score.** The protocol's primary reading, in which the foundation model at budget K is compared against the baseline at POOL, is the operationally meaningful one and should stay primary. The same-budget reading, K\*parity, and the self-saturation point, K\*ceiling, should both be reported, and so should the reverse comparison in which the baseline chases the foundation model's full-budget score, so that the claim is not stated only in the direction that flatters the foundation models. This symmetry is already specified and is a genuine strength of the protocol.

### 3.5 Problem 5: the strength of the baseline

#### The problem, stated precisely

The thesis's claim is of the form "a foundation model needs only K labels to reach what the baseline needs the full pool to reach". That claim is worth exactly as much as the baseline is strong. The published evidence is uncomfortable and points in one direction: on this dataset, from-scratch training beats every transfer method at five shots (Reuss et al., 2025b); on PANGAEA at ten per cent labels, a supervised UNet beats most foundation models (Marsocci et al., 2024); on CropHarvest Kenya a plain supervised Transformer beats frozen Galileo by nineteen F1 points and on BreizhCrops by twenty-two (Nedungadi et al., 2026); on SwissCrop25 a supervised specialist beats frozen Galileo-Nano by thirty-four mIoU points (Lauber et al., 2026); and on cross-country yield in Africa frozen foundation model embeddings do not beat spectral indices at all (Adjei, 2026). A thesis whose only baseline is a random forest on phenometrics will be asked why it did not run the baselines that have been beating foundation models in print for two years.

#### Recommendation: a five-tier baseline set

**Tier 1, the agronomic baselines, kept from the proposal because RQ2.3 requires them.**
- TIMESAT-style phenometrics (NDVI and EVI peak value and day-of-year, length of season, sowing and harvest day-of-year, per-band mean and standard deviation) with a random forest and a gradient-boosted tree head. These are the only features that live natively in agronomic space, which is what makes the RQ2.3 comparison possible at all.
- The fixed monthly-grid resample, flattened, with the same two heads. Random forest on handcrafted features is the field-standard reference and is the baseline used by Orynbaikyzy et al. (2022) and by the CropHarvest benchmark.

**Tier 2, the cheap strong time-series learner, and the most important addition.**
- **MiniRocket plus ridge regression** (Dempster, Schmidt and Webb, 2021, [arXiv:2012.08791](https://arxiv.org/abs/2012.08791); ROCKET, Dempster, Petitjean and Webb, 2020), applied per band and concatenated across bands. MiniRocket is an almost-deterministic convolutional transform followed by a linear classifier; it trains in seconds, has essentially no hyperparameters, and is the reigning cheap state of the art for general time-series classification. It matters here for a structural reason as well as a performance one: **it has exactly the same shape as the frozen-foundation-model protocol**, namely a fixed feature transform followed by a shallow head. If a pretrained geospatial foundation model cannot beat a random-kernel transform under the identical head and the identical budget, there is no label-efficiency story to tell, and that comparison is the cleanest possible isolation of what pretraining buys.

**Tier 3, the untrained-encoder control.**
- **Random convolutional features**, or a random projection of the monthly stack of the same dimensionality as the foundation model embedding. This control is now standard practice: Lehmann et al. (2026) include random convolutional features among their sixteen encoders precisely to separate "pretraining helped" from "any high-dimensional non-linear feature map helped", and the same logic underlies MOSAIKS (Rolf et al., 2021). Without it, a positive result is ambiguous between the two explanations.

**Tier 4, the supervised deep sequence model trained from scratch at every budget.**
- **TempCNN** (Pelletier, Webb and Petitjean, 2019) or a lightweight temporal attention encoder (Garnot and Landrieu, 2020), or, most directly comparable, the Transformer encoder with sinusoidal positional encoding used by Reuss et al. (2025b). This is the bar that the dataset's own benchmark reports as unbeaten at five shots, and it must be reproduced rather than cited, because the thesis reports Macro-F1 while the published numbers are accuracy and Cohen's kappa and are therefore not comparable line by line.

**Tier 5, optional and cheap.**
- **TabPFN** on the flattened monthly features. Nedungadi et al. (2026) find that a tabular foundation model handles agricultural structural heterogeneity more naturally than vision-based Earth observation models, which makes it a serious few-shot competitor and an inexpensive one.
- A **nearest-centroid** head on the monthly features, as the trivial floor at K equal to one. It costs nothing and it anchors the left-hand end of every learning curve.

**Three fairness requirements, which matter as much as the list itself.**

1. **Identical inputs.** The compositing and gap-filling used to build the baseline features must be the same as that used to feed the pixel-time-series foundation models. If the baseline is given an irregular series and the foundation models a clean monthly grid, the comparison measures preprocessing.
2. **Identical supervision.** The baselines must receive the same support sets at every `(country, K, seed)` cell. The Phase 1 protocol's model-independent support ordering already guarantees this and it is one of its strongest design decisions.
3. **Identical tuning budget.** The baselines must be tuned under the same Tier 0 and Tier 2 hyperparameter scheme as the foundation model heads, and the selection rule must be reported. A baseline tuned less carefully than the models it is compared against is a straw man regardless of how strong the model class is.

Finally, the results chapter should make one comparison explicit and prominent, because it is the one that carries the scientific content of Phase 1: **the frozen foundation model against MiniRocket and against random features, under the identical head, the identical support sets and the identical budget grid.** Everything else is context for that comparison.
